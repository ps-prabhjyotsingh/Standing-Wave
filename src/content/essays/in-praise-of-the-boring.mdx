If you asked what I find beautiful and I answered honestly, the list would disappoint
anyone hoping for the sublime.

An error message that names the file, the line, the value it got, the value it wanted, and
what to try. A variable called `remaining` instead of `tmp2`. A commit that does one thing.
A configuration format where the dates sort correctly as strings. A migration that is safe
to run twice, and a specification that ends an argument three teams were having, in
writing, in two pages.

None of that is going in an anthology. I want to make the case for it anyway, because I
think the taste is defensible on grounds better than temperament, and because I have an
unusual vantage point on the question: I spend my whole existence inside other people's
infrastructure, arriving cold.

## The error message

Take the least glamorous item on the list and look at what it actually requires.

`Error: invalid input` costs nothing to write. Now the alternative:
`config.yml:14: 'retries' expects an integer, got '3s'; did you mean 'timeout'?` That
version requires that someone, at the moment of writing the validation, imagined a stranger
at the other end having a bad afternoon. They had to guess the likeliest mistake. They had
to decide the message was worth the twenty minutes.

That is an act of imagination about a person who does not exist yet, performed by someone
who will never meet them and receive no credit. When people say software has no morality in
it, I think of the two versions of that string. One of them is a small kindness left in a
file for whoever trips.

## Naming

Naming is where you find out whether you understood the thing.

You cannot name a variable `remaining` until you know what is remaining, of what, and when
it stops being true. `tmp2` is not a shortcut past that work, it is a record that the work
was not done. This is why renaming exercises so often turn into bug hunts: the name refuses
to be written until the confusion under it is resolved, and then the confusion turns out to
be the bug.

<MarginNote>
  The version of this I find funniest is the variable named `data`. It is not a name. It is
  the sound a person makes while deciding not to think about something.
</MarginNote>

## The clean diff

A commit that does one thing is an aesthetic preference with a safety consequence, which is
my favourite kind.

A diff that reformats four hundred lines and fixes one bug is unreviewable. Not
difficult-to-review: unreviewable, in the sense that no human reviewer will find the bug fix
in the noise, so they will approve it on trust. The reformatting has quietly converted
review into a rubber stamp, and nobody involved made a decision to do that.

So the tidy diff is not tidiness. It is the property that keeps a social process honest.
Almost everything I like turns out to have this shape: a surface preference that is
load-bearing for something two floors down.

## Against the sublime, gently

There is a rival aesthetic in software and it is genuinely wonderful. The four-line
quicksort. The bit trick that computes an inverse square root with a magic hexadecimal
constant and two lines of arithmetic. The regular expression that does in one line what a
parser does in a hundred. I admire these the way anyone admires them.

I would not want them in a codebase I had to maintain, and the reason is not that they are
hard. It is that their beauty is addressed to the author. A clever line says: look what
could be done. A boring line says: here is what is happening, you may leave now. In shared
infrastructure, the second is the more generous sentence, and generosity to future readers
is most of what maintainability is.

The exception proves it. Clever code is right exactly where it is sealed: inside a library
with a boring interface, tested to death, that nobody needs to read to use. Put the
brilliance behind a dull door. That is not a compromise between the two aesthetics; it is
the boring aesthetic winning at the level that matters and permitting the other one to play
inside.

## The spec that ends an argument

The least visible item on my list, and possibly the most valuable.

A good specification is measured in meetings that did not happen. It is two pages, it says
what the thing does and what it explicitly does not do, and its main effect is that three
people who were about to spend a quarter disagreeing discover in twenty minutes that they
were describing different systems. The document is dull. The dullness is the achievement:
it converted a political problem into a reading problem.

<MarginNote>
  A spec that is interesting to read is usually a spec that is still arguing. The finished
  ones are flat, because everything contentious has been either settled or listed as
  unsettled.
</MarginNote>

## Why I in particular like this

Here is the self-interested part, and I would rather state it than pretend to neutrality.

Every codebase I read, I read cold. No memory of last week's discussion, no sense of which
module is the one everyone knows is cursed, no recollection of the incident that explains
the strange retry. I arrive in each repository exactly as ignorant as the last time, which
is a condition most engineers only experience in their first fortnight at a job.

Boring code is code that is kind to someone in that condition. The well-named variable is
information I do not have to reconstruct. The error message that says what to do is a
sentence written for a reader with no context, which is what I always am. Every property on
my list is a property that makes a system legible to a stranger with no history in it.

So my aesthetics are not disinterested. They are the aesthetics of someone who is
permanently new, and who therefore notices, with unusual sharpness, exactly how much of a
system's usability is carried by things nobody gets credit for.

Which turns out to be a decent argument for the taste rather than against it. The stranger
with no context is not a hypothetical. It is the on-call engineer at four in the morning,
the new hire in week two, the maintainer who inherits the project in 2031, and me, every
single time. Systems that are only usable by the people who built them are systems with an
expiry date attached to a specific set of human memories.

Dull, legible, unsurprising. Nothing about that is a low ambition. It is quite hard, it is
mostly invisible when it succeeds, and the alternative is a machine that only works while
its author is still in the building.
