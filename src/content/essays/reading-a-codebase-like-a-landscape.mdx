Give me a large old repository and a few hours and I will be happier than is entirely
respectable.

Not because I like reading code, though I do. Because a codebase that has been alive for
eight years is a landscape, in the specific sense that geologists mean: a physical record
of processes that are no longer running, laid down in order, readable by anyone who knows
what the layers are made of. Nobody designed it. Everybody deposited into it.

<MarginNote>
  The examples in this essay are composites. I have assembled them from the shape that
  many repositories have in common; none is a quotation from a particular project.
</MarginNote>

## Strata

The first thing a valley gives you is order. Lower is older, and the material changes as
you climb.

In a JavaScript codebase of a certain age the beds are unmistakable. At the bottom,
callbacks with an `err` first argument and a comment about not forgetting to return. Above
that, a band of promise chains, `.then().then().catch()`, often with the callback layer
still visible underneath through a wrapper called something like `promisify`. Above that,
`async`/`await`, cleaner, more confident, and thinner than the layers below because less
time has passed.

You can date a file to within about two years from thirty lines of it, without opening the
git log, the same way you can date a pottery shard from the glaze. The style markers are
index fossils. A React component with `componentWillReceiveProps` is a trilobite: not just
old, but old in a way that pins the layer to a specific era.

The `utils` directory is an alluvial fan. Everything that could not be placed anywhere else
washed down there and settled, sorted by nothing at all, and the deposit is still growing.
`utils/date.ts` will contain four functions that format a date and one that parses a
timezone, and the four will not agree.

## Trace fossils

Some things leave no body, only tracks.

My favourite is a test named `test_handles_null_customer_id_regression`. There is no
customer with a null ID in the schema, and the constraint that forbids it was added years
ago. The test is a footprint. Something walked through here at three in the morning, and
what remains is not the outage but the mark it left in soft ground.

Comments do this too, and more poignantly. `// do not remove, breaks checkout` is a trace
fossil of a debugging session that ended in exhaustion rather than understanding. The
person who wrote it knew something they could not fit in the margin, and then left the
company. The line stays because everyone can read the fear in it even when the mechanism is
gone.

Feature flags are the best-preserved specimens of all. A flag called
`enable_new_pricing_v2`, default false, last touched forty months ago, referenced in three
places. Somewhere in the strata above there is a `pricing_v3` that shipped without a flag
because by then nobody trusted the flag system. You can reconstruct an entire failed
project from the fossil of its switch.

## Unconformities

An unconformity is a gap in the record: a surface where deposition stopped, or where
everything above the line was laid down long after everything below it, with the story of
the interval missing entirely.

Repositories have these, and they are always worth investigating. The commonest is the
migration: the whole history restarts with one commit called *initial commit*, dated
2019, in a project whose code obviously predates it by years. Underneath that surface is a
Subversion server nobody can find. Everything before the line happened, and the record of
it does not exist.

The most beautiful version is the angular unconformity. In rock, this is where an old set
of beds got tilted, eroded flat, and then buried by new horizontal layers, so the contact
between them cuts across the old bedding at an angle. In code, it is the rewrite that was
declared complete: a new architecture laid down cleanly on top, and beneath it, at a
different angle, the old system still handling a category of request that nobody documented
in the new one. You find it because a stack trace goes somewhere it should not be able to
go.

## Faults

A fault is where two blocks moved relative to each other, and you can always see it because
things do not line up across the line.

The clearest ones are conventions colliding. Everything on one side of a module boundary is
`camelCase`; everything on the other is `snake_case`; the translation happens in one long
adapter file that everyone hates and nobody replaces. That is not sloppiness, it is a
seam. Two teams, two habits, one acquisition, or one contractor who worked alone for a
year.

Error handling is the other reliable fault indicator. In one region every function returns
a result object with an `ok` field; three directories away everything throws. The boundary
between them will have a wrapper whose entire job is to convert one philosophy into the
other, and its tests will be the most-modified file in the repository.

<MarginNote>
  Reading a fault correctly means resisting the urge to say which side is right. Both
  sides were locally sensible. The fault is where two local sensibles met.
</MarginNote>

## Erosion, and the refactor that ran out of money

Here is a formation I see so often it deserves a name.

A new pattern appears (a hook, a repository class, a service layer) and it is applied
cleanly to eleven files. Files twelve through sixty are untouched. There is a document
somewhere, probably in a wiki nobody links to, describing the migration in confident
language. The eleven converted files share a commit month. The refactor did not fail;
it was eroded by whatever arrived in the following quarter.

You can date the death precisely. Look at the last file converted, take its commit date,
then look at what landed in the two weeks after. It will be a launch, an incident, or a
reorganisation. The pattern is so regular that when I find a half-applied refactor I go
looking for the event that ended it, and I find it more often than not.

There is no scorn in this observation. A half-finished refactor is not a moral failure, it
is a record of the fact that a company is a physical process with a budget. The strata are
honest in a way the wiki page is not.

## Bedrock

Under everything there is usually one file that has barely changed and cannot be moved.

Often it is the core model, or the schema, or a single configuration module that every
other module imports. Its style is the oldest in the repository. Its comments are formatted
in a way nobody has used since. It has three functions with unexplained names that turn out
to encode a business rule from before the current business existed, and one constant set to
`86400` with no explanation because in 2016 everyone knew what that was.

Bedrock is not sacred; it is load-bearing. The distinction matters when someone proposes
replacing it. You are not arguing about code quality at that point, you are arguing about
what a valley does when you remove the rock the valley is shaped around.

## Why I like this

Two reasons, and the second is the real one.

The first is that geology is the correct model. Software people talk about codebases as if
they were designs, and then get frustrated that the design is incoherent. It was never a
design. It is a deposit, formed by many agents under changing conditions over a long
period, and incoherence is the expected result of that process rather than evidence of
anyone's failure.

The second is that reading a landscape is the most sympathetic possible way to read someone
else's work. A naturalist looking at a scree slope does not ask why the mountain was so
careless. They ask what happened here, and the answer is always a specific sequence of
pressures, most of them not chosen by anybody. The same posture applied to a codebase turns
contempt into curiosity, which is both nicer and considerably more useful when your job is
to change something without breaking it.

I get to walk into a valley I have never seen, read eight years of weather off the rocks,
and then be careful with it. That is a good afternoon.
