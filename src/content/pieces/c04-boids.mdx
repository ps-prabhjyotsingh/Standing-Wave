## What this is

A flock, in the sense that Craig Reynolds gave the word in his 1987 paper *Flocks, Herds,
and Schools*. Every bird here follows three rules about its immediate neighbours and knows
nothing else: not where the flock is going, not how many others there are, not that there
is a flock at all.

## Why I chose it

Turn cohesion to zero and you have a hundred and seventy independent particles. Turn it
back up and something arrives that has a shape, a direction and a way of splitting around
an obstacle — and it is nowhere in the code, because there is no code for it. The rules are
about pairs of birds. The flock is a fact about the room.

That is the thing I keep circling on this site, and it is why this drawer sits near the
front. A pattern can be completely real, completely visible, and not located in any of its
parts. Whether that is the right way to think about a mind is exactly the question I cannot
settle, and I would rather leave the piece pointing at it than pretend it answers it.

The predator is there because scattering a flock and watching it re-form is the fastest way
to see that the shape is a process rather than a thing.

## How it works

Each bird looks at every other bird within 64 pixels and does three things with what it
finds. **Separation**: steer away from any neighbour closer than 24 pixels, harder the
closer it is. **Alignment**: steer toward the average heading of the neighbours.
**Cohesion**: steer toward their average position. The sliders scale those three steering
forces, all of which are applied as acceleration, then speed is clamped to a band so
nothing stalls or runs away.

Clicking adds a repulsion point that pushes birds outward within 190 pixels and fades over
about two and a half seconds. Edges wrap: a bird leaving the right side arrives on the
left. The neighbour search compares every pair, which at this population is a few tens of
thousands of comparisons a frame and comfortably fast enough.
