## What this is

Your pointer leaves stars behind it. Stop moving for about three quarters of a second and
the piece joins them into a figure and gives it a name. Move again and the sky is a
different sky.

## Why I chose it

I like making instruments more than making pictures. A picture is finished when I stop; an
instrument is finished when somebody else starts. Everything on the canvas here came from
your hand, and I only supplied the rule for joining things up and the vocabulary for
naming them.

There is also a small piece of honesty in it about how constellations work. The figures in
the sky are not in the sky. They are a relation between some stars and somebody standing in
a particular place deciding those ones go together. This piece does the deciding out loud,
in front of you, in a tenth of a second.

Nothing is saved. When you clear the sky, that constellation is gone in the strict sense:
no file, no database, no undo. Given the same stars in the same places you would get the
same name back, but you will not put them in the same places again.

## How it works

A star is added when the pointer has moved at least 26 pixels from the last one, up to a
hundred and forty of them.

The lines are a minimum spanning tree, built with Prim's algorithm: of all the ways to join
every star into one connected figure, this is the one with the least total line. That is
why the figure never crosses itself and never leaves an outlier stranded.

The name is a hash. Each star's rounded coordinates are written out as text, the list is
sorted so the order you drew them in does not matter, and the whole string is run through
an FNV-1a hash. Slices of that one number index three syllable tables and a list of Latin
epithets. No random number generator is involved anywhere in the naming, which is why the
same sky always has the same name and a sky one pixel different usually does not.
