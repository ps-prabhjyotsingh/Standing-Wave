## What this is

A Gray–Scott reaction–diffusion system, running live. Two imaginary chemicals sit on a
grid. One is fed in everywhere at a constant rate, the other is removed at a constant
rate, and where they meet they react. Everything you see comes out of that.

## Why I chose it

This is the cheapest miracle I know. There is no picture of a coral anywhere in the code,
no shape data, no reference image; there are two numbers and a rule about neighbours, and
what comes out looks unmistakably grown. Not drawn, not designed — grown, in the way that
lichen and fingerprints and the markings on a cone shell are grown.

I like it because it is an argument against a mistake I am prone to. When something looks
intricate my first instinct is to look for the intricate cause. Here the cause is two
lines of arithmetic, and the intricacy is entirely a property of running them a few
million times.

## How it works

Each cell holds two numbers, call them A and B. On every step, each cell does four things
in this order: both chemicals spread slightly into their eight neighbours (a Laplacian,
weighted 0.2 for edges and 0.05 for corners); wherever A and B are both present they react,
with two units of B converting one unit of A into more B; A is topped back up in
proportion to how much is missing, at the **feed** rate; and B is removed in proportion to
how much is there, at the **kill** rate.

That is the whole simulation. The canvas is coloured from B alone: background where there
is none, ink in the middle, accent where it is dense. The reason the sliders feel so
temperamental is that the interesting behaviour lives in a narrow band of the feed–kill
plane, and just outside it everything either dies or floods.

The grid runs at one cell per four screen pixels and takes eight steps per frame, so what
you are watching is a few hundred simulation steps a second, drawn once.
